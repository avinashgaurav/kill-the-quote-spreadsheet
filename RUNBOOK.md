# The one rule

In Part 1 you will write a new enquiry. **Do not press the button that sends it
to suppliers.**

Why: the supplier replies we have on file answer the IT hardware enquiry. If you
send a new one, the app switches to showing the new one, and it has no replies,
so the grid goes blank.

You still show everything about drafting. You just don't push that enquiry out.

# Part 1 · Draft an enquiry · 2 minutes

### What you do

**Click `Draft`.**

**Type this** (or anything you like, in your own words):

> I need to refresh IT hardware for 14 retail sites in Tamil Nadu. Laptops for
> the store managers, a small server per region, network switches. Delivered in
> phases from March.

**Click the `Send` button.** Not the Enter key.

### What happens

It asks you a few questions — only about things it genuinely cannot guess, like
quantities and the warranty term. Answer them briefly.

Then four documents appear on the right: **scope, line items, questionnaire,
terms.** Each one downloadable.

**Amber warnings appear next to them.** These are the gate.

### What to say

> "It asks about what it can't guess and assumes sensible defaults for the rest,
> and tells me which it assumed. It will not invent a price. It has no view on
> what things cost."

Point at a warning about a unit.

> "This is the whole product in one screen. A line that says 'box' without
> saying how many are inside it. One supplier prices the box, the next prices a
> single piece, and the second bid looks five times cheaper while being dearer.
> **Both of them answered the question I actually asked.** Nothing downstream
> can fix that, so it gets fixed here."

> "And I can override it, by typing a reason. That reason stays on the enquiry
> and turns up in the award note. That's deliberate — an unclickable button just
> gets worked around by editing the draft until the check stops firing, and that
> leaves no record at all."

**Now move on. Do not send.**

# Part 2 · Send it to suppliers · 1 minute

### What you do

**Click `Responses`.**

You see a roster of ten suppliers, five pre-ticked.

**Untick one that is ticked.** **Tick one that is not** (Trilok, Meghdoot,
Arunoday, Kadamba or Sahyadri).

**Click `Whatsapp`.**

**Click `Send to N suppliers`.**

### What happens

Replies arrive one at a time, in a few seconds each. Each one says what was read
— "xlsx, 30 lines priced", "questionnaire, 10 answers".

The supplier you ticked who has nothing on file says: **"no response. Invited,
sent nothing."**

### What to say

Before you press send:

> "WhatsApp can't carry an attachment. So the pack goes out as a link, the
> supplier who gets a link on their phone is the one who replies with a
> photograph, and on the way back **their certificate doesn't arrive either.**"

Point at the supplier who sent nothing:

> "That's not a gap in my dataset. You invite ten and five answer. That state is
> what the chase step exists for."

Say this once, carefully:

> "**Exactly one thing here is faked: the delivery.** Nothing was emailed and no
> mailbox was polled. Everything that arrived was then read for real. A button
> that makes prepared answers appear is a scripted demo. A button that makes
> real documents arrive, which are then genuinely read, is a stubbed transport."

# Part 4 · The money moment · 90 seconds

### What to say, pointing at the top

> "Three point eight eight crore. And it's labelled **'cheapest, ignoring the
> questionnaire'**, because it includes suppliers who can't be awarded at any
> price. It stays on screen, because hiding it would be its own kind of
> dishonesty.
>
> The real number is **four point oh five crore**. Doing this properly costs
> **sixteen point seven lakh, four point three per cent.** The cheaper number
> was never available."

### Then the certificate

**In the Ask box, type:**

> Is Vector's ISO 27001 certificate actually valid?

**While it answers:** click the **Vector** column header, then click
**`VDS_ISO27001.pdf`** in the panel.

Say this slowly. It's the best 90 seconds you have:

> "Vector answered **Yes**. They attached a certificate.
>
> Their questionnaire response is a **form**. A form has room for a filename and
> nothing else. So if you only read the form, you get 'answered yes, attached
> something, nothing contradicts it' — **a pass.**
>
> The contradiction is inside the PDF. So the system opens the attachment and
> reads it in its own right: **ISO/IEC 27001:2013, expired the thirtieth of
> November 2025.** I asked for the 2022 revision.
>
> And where the form and the document disagree, **the document governs.** A model
> asked 'did they pass?' says yes, because they said yes. Comparing a date to
> today isn't a judgement call, so code does it — and the verdict is recomputed
> on every read, never stored. A certificate that expires next week changes the
> answer without anybody editing a row."

**Click `Ask them to resolve these 6`.**

> "So the failure has an action attached to it, not just a red badge."

### One more, worth 10 seconds

Click the **Cygnus** or **Helios** column header. Both say **NOT ASSESSED**.

> "Neither of these two ever sent a questionnaire. They're carried as eligible,
> not excluded — dropping a real bid because nobody chased a document is the more
> expensive mistake. But the screen never calls that a pass."

**Watch for this in Part 5.** When you ask the first question, the analyst
volunteers the same caveat itself, unprompted:

> *"the questionnaires for Cygnus and Helios have not been assessed. They are
> included because unread questionnaires are treated as eligible by default to
> avoid dropping valid bids, but they have not actually cleared the quality
> checks yet."*

If it says that, **point at it and stop talking.** The tool correcting your
question is worth more than you explaining the rule.

# Part 5 · Ask it things · 3 minutes

Type each one. **Don't click the suggested buttons** — typing makes it obvious
nothing is canned.

**1.**
> Split it cheapest per line, but only among suppliers who cleared the quality questionnaire

> "It computes. It doesn't add up in prose. There's deliberately no calculator
> tool you can hand two numbers to."

**2.**
> What did that change against taking the cheapest from anyone?

> "Watch it lead with coverage before money, unprompted."

**3.**
> Which numbers are you least sure about, biggest rupee impact first?

> "Ordered by money, not by count."

**4.**
> What would we save by dropping the ISO 27001 requirement?

Let it answer, then:

> "I asked a question with an uncomfortable answer, and it computed it and told
> me what I'd be giving up."

**5.**
> Chart the split award by supplier

**6.**
> Draft the award recommendation and say what it rests on

**Click `Award note`.** Scroll to the chase section.

> "'Their questionnaire is missing' and 'we asked on the tenth, gave them until
> the sixteenth, and nothing came back' are **different facts.** Only the second
> one lets a buyer award around a supplier and defend it to a CFO."

**7.** Now ask it something it can't do:
> Which of these suppliers has the best reputation in the market?

> "It refuses, and says why. It answers from the extracted data or it doesn't
> answer. **That refusal is the most persuasive thing in this recording.**"

# Part 6 · Where the AI is · 30 seconds

**Click `where the model is used`** on the trust bar.

> "Six model calls. Nothing else in this tool talks to a model. And beside each
> one, what it **may not** do: drafting may not invent a price, reading may not
> convert or rank, the questionnaire reader may not decide whether anybody
> passed, the analyst may not compute at all.
>
> **The model reads. The code counts.** Two implementations of that calculator,
> one in Python and one in TypeScript, proven to agree on all hundred and fifty
> cells by four hundred and forty-two assertions. You can't do that to a model.
>
> What it costs me: my code returns 'cannot resolve' where a model would have
> improvised. **A plausible price is worse than an admitted gap**, because a gap
> gets chased and a plausible price gets awarded."

# Part 7 · Break it on purpose · 60 seconds

**Do this last. Don't cut it.**

**Go to `Responses`. Drag any random file from your Downloads in.**

> "It refuses rather than guessing, because a price in the wrong column is a
> mistake nobody downstream can detect."

**Name a supplier.** A new column appears, marked NOT ASSESSED.

**Drag in something absurd** — a screenshot, a zip.

> "Refused with instructions rather than half-read. And a read that finds
> nothing throws, rather than quietly putting an empty column in front of
> somebody who'd read it as 'this supplier didn't quote'."

### Close on this

> "One last thing, and it's the honest answer to 'what does it show you when it
> isn't sure'.
>
> I read the same rate card as five photographs of falling quality. The fourth
> came back **fifty-two per cent accurate, at nought point nine confidence on
> every single wrong digit.** Fifty-nine thousand nine hundred, for fifty-seven
> thousand nine hundred.
>
> That's the exact failure this whole design exists to prevent, and on that
> photograph it showed me certainty. My own test harness had missed it, because
> it was only comparing the easiest and hardest images and the bad one was in the
> middle. It scores every row now.
>
> I'd rather hand you a measured failure than an unmeasured claim."
